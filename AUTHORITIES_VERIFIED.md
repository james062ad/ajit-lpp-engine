## Mann v Carnell (1999) 201 CLR 1; [1999] HCA 66

- Verified: 22 Jul 2026, by GJML, from: High Court of Australia,
  official judgment PDF, Mann v Carnell [1999] HCA 66
  (Gleeson CJ, Gaudron, Gummow and Callinan JJ).

- Propositions confirmed against AU-WAIV-01/02, AU-CONF-02:
  CONFIRMED, subject to the qualification noted below:

  1. Waiver occurs where the privilege holder's conduct is inconsistent with
     maintaining the confidentiality that legal professional privilege exists
     to protect. The inquiry is objective; waiver may be imputed even though
     loss of privilege was not subjectively intended.

  2. Disclosure for a limited and specific purpose, including disclosure on
     terms of confidentiality, does not necessarily waive privilege. The
     question remains whether the disclosure was inconsistent with maintaining
     the confidentiality protected by the privilege.

  3. The bare fact of voluntary disclosure to a third party does not, by
     itself, necessarily establish waiver. It is the relevant inconsistency,
     assessed in the circumstances and where necessary informed by
     considerations of fairness, that effects waiver.

- Pinpoints: [28]–[32], particularly [28]–[30]; see also [34]–[35]
  concerning application of the inconsistency test to the disclosure in issue.

- Notes: AU-WAIV-01 and AU-WAIV-02 accurately reflect the majority's
  inconsistency test. AU-CONF-02 should use “does not necessarily waive” or
  “is not of itself sufficient to establish waiver”, rather than “waiver is
  never inferred from the bare fact of disclosure”. The latter formulation is
  unnecessarily absolute and does not reproduce the Court's language precisely.

Propositions confirmed against AU-PARTY-01:
CONFIRMED, subject to the qualification below:
A document prepared by a third-party adviser may attract legal advice
privilege even though the third party is not the client’s agent for the
purpose of communicating with the lawyer.
The controlling question is whether the document was brought into
existence for the dominant purpose of the client obtaining legal advice,
including by communicating the document to the client’s legal adviser.
The absence of a lawyer as a direct party to the communication is not
decisive where the third-party document was prepared for provision to the
lawyer as part of the process of obtaining legal advice.
The case establishes that such a document is capable of attracting
privilege. It does not establish that every report commissioned from an
accountant or other adviser is privileged; the dominant purpose must be
established from the evidence.
Pinpoints:
Finn J at [1], [19]–[21], [30]–[41] and [48]–[51];
Merkel J at [52];
Stone J at [86]–[105], particularly [105].
Notes:
AU-PARTY-01 is substantially accurate if it focuses on the client’s dominant
purpose in procuring the document. It should not require the third party to
have been a formal communication agent. It should also avoid stating that the
PricewaterhouseCoopers report was finally held privileged: the Full Court
established the governing principle and remitted the factual privilege
determination.
Attorney-General (NT) v Kearney (1985) 159 CLR 500; [1985] HCA 60

Propositions confirmed against AU-CONF-04:
PARTLY CONFIRMED — the substantive exception is confirmed, but the engine’s
“recorded finding” threshold is a deliberately stricter operational rule:
Legal professional privilege does not protect communications made in
furtherance of an illegal or improper purpose.
“Improper purpose” is not confined to fraud or an indictable criminal
offence. It may include a deliberate misuse or abuse of statutory power.
In Kearney, the alleged purpose concerned the use of regulation-making
power to obstruct or defeat Aboriginal land claims. The case therefore
supports an exception extending beyond conventional crime-fraud facts.
A bare allegation of impropriety is insufficient. There must be evidence
capable of supporting a prima facie case that the communication was made
in furtherance of the improper purpose.
Pinpoints:
Gibbs CJ at 514–515 CLR;
Mason and Brennan JJ at 525–527 CLR;
Deane J at 531–532 CLR;
Dawson J at 544–545 CLR.
Notes:
AU-CONF-04 may properly demand a recorded judicial or evidentiary finding
before automatically denying privilege, but that is an engine safeguard, not
the precise threshold stated in Kearney. The case itself speaks in terms of
evidence establishing a prima facie case, not necessarily a final finding of
illegality or impropriety.
Waterford v Commonwealth (1987) 163 CLR 54; [1987] HCA 25
Verified: 22 Jul 2026, by: OpenAI-assisted authority review, from:
High Court of Australia judgment, Waterford v Commonwealth
(1987) 163 CLR 54; [1987] HCA 25.
Propositions confirmed against AU-PARTY-02/03:
CONFIRMED, with an important qualification concerning professional status:
Legal professional privilege may attach to communications between a
government department or agency and a salaried government legal adviser.
The existence of an employment relationship between client and lawyer
does not itself prevent the formation of the professional lawyer-client
relationship required for privilege.
The adviser must be acting in a professional legal capacity and with the
independence required of a legal adviser. Privilege does not arise merely
because an employee’s position has “legal” in its title.
Capacity must be assessed by reference to the particular communication
and engagement, including whether legal advice, rather than administrative,
policy or commercial assistance, was being provided.
Pinpoints:
Mason and Wilson JJ at 61–62 CLR;
Brennan J at 69–72 CLR;
Deane J at 77–78 CLR;
Dawson J at 85–86 CLR.
Notes:
The judgments do not reduce the qualification issue to one universal verbal
formula. Mason and Wilson JJ focus on the professional lawyer-client
relationship; Brennan J emphasises professional competence and independence;
Deane J discusses legal professional capacity; and Dawson J addresses the
position of salaried government lawyers in the statutory context.
AU-PARTY-02/03 should therefore require evidence of professional legal
capacity and independence for the particular engagement. A separate
lawyer_qualification_basis field would accurately preserve the differences
among the judgments.
Daniels Corporation International Pty Ltd v Australian Competition and Consumer Commission
(2002) 213 CLR 543; [2002] HCA 49
Verified: 22 Jul 2026, by: OpenAI-assisted authority review, from:
High Court of Australia judgment, Daniels Corporation International Pty Ltd
v Australian Competition and Consumer Commission [2002] HCA 49.
Propositions confirmed against AU-STAT-01:
CONFIRMED:
Legal professional privilege is an important substantive common-law
immunity or right and is not merely a rule controlling the admissibility
of evidence at trial.
A statute will not be construed as abolishing or overriding legal
professional privilege unless that result is required by express words or
by necessary implication.
Section 155 of the Trade Practices Act 1974 (Cth) did not expressly
abrogate privilege, and abrogation was not necessarily implied by the
statutory scheme.
Accordingly, legal professional privilege remained a valid answer to a
requirement under s 155 to produce privileged documents.
Pinpoints:
Gleeson CJ, Gaudron, Gummow and Hayne JJ at [9]–[11] and [30]–[35],
particularly [11] and [32];
McHugh J at [43]–[44];
Kirby J at [85]–[89] and [106]–[108];
Callinan J at [132]–[134].
Notes:
AU-STAT-01 accurately states the governing principle. For precision, use
“express words or necessary implication”, rather than merely “clear words”.
The rule should also distinguish abrogation from regulation of the manner in
which privilege is asserted.
Commissioner of Australian Federal Police v Propend Finance Pty Ltd

Propositions confirmed against AU-COPY-01:
CONFIRMED BY MAJORITY, but the rule must identify the privileged purpose of
making the copy:
A copy of an otherwise non-privileged original document may itself attract
legal professional privilege where the copy was made for the dominant
purpose of obtaining legal advice or use in existing or anticipated
litigation.
Privilege attaches to the copy because of the purpose for which the copy
was created. It does not retrospectively make the original document
privileged.
The protection ordinarily applies in the hands of the client or lawyer
holding the purpose-created copy. It does not confer a general immunity
over all other copies or the original wherever found.
The majority supporting protection for purpose-created copies comprised
Brennan CJ, Gaudron, McHugh, Gummow and Kirby JJ. Dawson and Toohey JJ
rejected that approach.
Pinpoints:
Brennan CJ at 509–510 CLR;
Gaudron J at 537–538 CLR;
McHugh J at 556–558 CLR;
Gummow J at 585–586 CLR;
Kirby J at 609–611 CLR;
Dawson J dissenting at 526–527 CLR;
Toohey J dissenting at 531–533 CLR.
Notes:
AU-COPY-01 is accurate if it says that the copy “may” attract privilege and
expressly ties protection to the dominant purpose for which that particular
copy was made. It should not say that copying an unprivileged document makes
its contents generally privileged.
The case arose in a search-warrant and seizure context, but the majority’s
reasoning establishes the broader privilege principle concerning
purpose-created copies.
Expense Reduction Analysts Group Pty Ltd v Armstrong Strategic Management
and Marketing Pty Ltd (2013) 250 CLR 303; [2013] HCA 46
Verified: 22 Jul 2026, by: OpenAI-assisted authority review, from:
High Court of Australia official judgment, Expense Reduction Analysts Group
Pty Ltd v Armstrong Strategic Management and Marketing Pty Ltd
[2013] HCA 46.
Propositions confirmed against the AU-WAIV inadvertent-disclosure route:
CONFIRMED, with the case-management qualification below:
Inadvertent production of privileged documents during discovery does not,
of itself, waive privilege.
Where the disclosure was the result of an obvious mistake and the
disclosing party promptly sought correction, the conduct was not
inconsistent with maintaining the confidentiality protected by privilege.
The receiving solicitor was required to recognise the apparent mistake,
refrain from taking advantage of it and facilitate the return of the
documents.
The court could permit correction of the discovery list, order return or
deletion of the documents and restrain their use through its procedural
and case-management powers.
Pinpoints:
[18]–[21] concerning the mistake and the receiving solicitors’ response;
[28]–[33] concerning waiver and application of Mann v Carnell;
[34]–[38] concerning correction of discovery and case-management powers;
[39]–[45] concerning practitioners’ obligations and the proper disposition.
Notes:
The case is not merely a freestanding statement that inadvertent disclosure
can never waive privilege. The conclusion depended on the objective
inconsistency test, the evident mistake, prompt corrective action, the
discovery context and the court’s case-management powers.
The safest engine formulation is:
“Inadvertent disclosure in discovery does not of itself effect waiver.
Determine whether the privilege holder’s conduct, considered objectively and
including the response after discovering the mistake, was inconsistent with
maintaining confidentiality.”

PARTLY CONFIRMED, but the engine’s present description should be narrowed:
The material concerned approximately 31 documents generated through the
consumer version of Anthropic’s Claude platform.
The court held that the communications were not protected by
attorney-client privilege because Claude was not an attorney, the relevant
communications were not confidential communications with counsel and the
user had disclosed information to Claude and Anthropic as third parties.
The court relied on Anthropic’s applicable user terms or privacy policy,
under which user content could be handled or disclosed to third parties,
including governmental or regulatory authorities. On those facts, the
defendant lacked a reasonable expectation of confidentiality.
The court also rejected work-product protection, although that analysis
must not be imported uncritically into Australian privilege law.
